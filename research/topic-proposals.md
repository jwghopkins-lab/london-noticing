# Ten topic proposals — central London

Research pass, 16 Aug 2026. No stop content written yet. The user picks 5 of these 10;
only then do we choose the 6 insights per topic and hand-author the 10 routes.

Rules this was written against: each topic needs at least 6 genuinely non-obvious
insights, carried where possible by free museums and churches, tight enough
geographically that 18 stops make a walk rather than a march.

---

## 1. Roman London

Londinium is still here. Most of it is below you, some of it is at eye level.

Non-obvious insights:
- The Temple of Mithras was dug up in 1954, moved about 100 metres so an office
  could be built, then in 2017 put back roughly where it was found, seven metres
  down. It has been relocated twice in living memory.
- Guildhall Yard has a dark curved line laid into the paving. It marks the outline
  of the Roman amphitheatre directly underneath. People walk across the arena floor
  all day without looking down.
- The amphitheatre was only found in 1988, when they dug foundations for the new
  art gallery. It had been lost for 1,600 years under the middle of the City.
- All Hallows by the Tower has a Roman tessellated floor in the crypt and a Saxon
  arch built out of recycled Roman tiles.
- The wall at Tower Hill is Roman at the bottom and medieval at the top. You can
  see the join, and the Trajan statue in front of it is a 20th-century copy.
- Noble Street has an open stretch of the wall and a corner turret of the Roman
  fort, unfenced, at pavement level, and almost nobody stops.
- Bishopsgate, Aldgate, Newgate and Ludgate are named after Roman gates. The roads
  still go where they go because of where those gates were.
- London Stone sits behind glass in a wall on Cannon Street. Nobody is certain
  what it was for.

Free sites: London Mithraeum (free, timed ticket, Tue-Sat), Guildhall Art Gallery
and the amphitheatre (free), All Hallows by the Tower (free), St Bride's crypt (free).
London Museum at Smithfield opens 28 Nov 2026 — after any near-term POC.

Spread: Tower Hill, Aldgate, Bank/Walbrook, Guildhall, London Wall, Noble Street,
Barbican. A clean line of about 2 km.

## 2. The Great Fire and the City Wren Rebuilt

Four days in 1666 and the fifty years of building that followed.

Non-obvious insights:
- The Monument is 61.5 metres tall, and that is exactly how far it stands from the
  bakery where the fire started. Hooke also built a zenith telescope into the shaft,
  so it is a scientific instrument as well as a column.
- The Golden Boy of Pye Corner at Cock Lane marks where the fire stopped. The
  inscription blames gluttony, because it started in Pudding Lane and stopped at Pye
  Corner, and Londoners decided that was a message.
- The tiered wedding cake is a copy of St Bride's spire. A Fleet Street baker made
  the first one.
- Wren gave every church a different steeple on purpose. From street level you
  mostly saw the spire, so that is where the design effort went.
- St Magnus the Martyr stood at the foot of old London Bridge. The path through its
  porch was the pavement of the bridge. There is a large model of the medieval
  bridge inside.
- Dozens of burnt churches were never rebuilt. The small gardens dotted around the
  City are their churchyards.
- St Mary Aldermanbury was shipped to Missouri stone by stone in 1966 and rebuilt
  there. The footprint is still in the City as a garden.
- St Dunstan-in-the-East survived the Fire, was patched by Wren, then lost to the
  Blitz. It is now a ruin garden.

Free sites: St Bride's, St Mary-le-Bow, St Magnus the Martyr, St Stephen Walbrook,
St Dunstan-in-the-East garden. The Monument charges a small fee — either skip the
climb or treat the base as the stop.

Spread: Monument, St Magnus, Bank, Cheapside, St Paul's, St Bride's, Cock Lane.
About 2.5 km.

## 3. London's Lost Rivers and the Old Waterfront

The water is still running. It is just under the road.

Non-obvious insights:
- Kneel at the grating in Ray Street, Clerkenwell, outside the Coach and Horses,
  and you can hear the River Fleet running below. It is loud.
- Farringdon Road sits in a valley because the valley is the river. Once you see
  the slope you cannot unsee it.
- Holborn Viaduct exists to bridge that valley. Standing on Holborn you are on a
  bridge over a river you cannot see.
- The Walbrook gave its name to the street. The Roman temple was built on its bank,
  which is why the temple is where it is.
- The York Watergate in Victoria Embankment Gardens was a river gate. It now stands
  about 150 metres from the water, stranded when Bazalgette reclaimed the land in
  the 1860s. It shows exactly how much river was taken.
- The Aldgate Pump killed people. Its water came down from Hampstead through new
  graveyards, and it was blamed for hundreds of deaths before it was cut off in 1876.
  The spout is a wolf's head.
- Marylebone Lane bends the way it does because it follows the buried Tyburn.
- At low tide you can walk down stairs to the Thames foreshore and stand on the
  riverbed in the middle of the city.

Free sites: London Mithraeum, St Bride's, St Etheldreda in Ely Place.

Spread: Clerkenwell, Farringdon, Holborn Viaduct, Fleet Street, Blackfriars,
Walbrook, Embankment. About 3 km and it makes a natural line.

## 4. Plague, Death and the Buried City

Where the bodies are, and who was allowed a headstone.

Non-obvious insights:
- Cross Bones in Southwark is an unconsecrated graveyard for the women who worked
  in the Bishop of Winchester's licensed brothels. The gates are covered in ribbons
  left by strangers.
- The gateway of St Olave Hart Street is topped with carved skulls. Dickens called
  it Saint Ghastly Grim. Pepys is buried inside.
- There is a Black Death mass grave under Charterhouse Square. Crossrail confirmed
  it in 2013, and the plague bacterium was sequenced from the teeth.
- Bunhill Fields is outside the old City wall because dissenters were not allowed
  in. Blake, Defoe and Bunyan are all there.
- Postman's Park has ceramic tablets naming ordinary people who died saving someone
  else. Read three of them and you will go quiet.
- More than 3,000 skeletons were lifted from the Bedlam burial ground at Liverpool
  Street for Crossrail.
- Most plague victims were not thrown into pits. Parish registers show ordinary
  burials right up until the system was overwhelmed.
- St Bartholomew the Great survived the Fire and later did time as a printworks
  and a blacksmith's forge.

Free sites: Postman's Park, Bunhill Fields, St Olave Hart Street, Charterhouse
Square garden, St Botolph Aldgate. Cross Bones is free but only opens Wed-Fri and
the first Saturday, 12-2pm — a hard operational constraint. St Bartholomew the
Great charges visitors.

Spread: Southwark, Bank, Aldgate, Smithfield, Barbican, Bunhill. Wide, and Cross
Bones pulls it over the river.

## 5. The Blitz and the Scars of War

Damage that was left on purpose, and the city that grew out of the holes.

Non-obvious insights:
- The sphinx by Cleopatra's Needle is gouged with shrapnel from a German raid in
  September 1917. It was never repaired, and there is a plaque explaining that the
  damage was kept deliberately.
- St Dunstan-in-the-East was bombed and left as a ruin. Wren's tower stood. It is
  now one of the best gardens in the City and most people find it by accident.
- St Clement Danes was gutted in 1941 and rebuilt as the RAF church. Hundreds of
  squadron badges are set into the floor.
- The Barbican exists because the area was flattened. St Giles Cripplegate standing
  alone in the middle of it is the survivor, not the addition.
- The bombing is why we can see Roman London. The Temple of Mithras turned up in a
  bomb site, and long stretches of the wall are visible because the buildings in
  front of them were destroyed.
- St Paul's survived because volunteers slept in the dome and threw incendiaries off
  the roof.
- The Cenotaph is empty and undecorated by design, and it was meant to be temporary.
- The Gherkin stands on the site of the Baltic Exchange, wrecked by an IRA bomb in
  1992. Not the Blitz, but the same lesson about how the skyline gets made.

Free sites: St Clement Danes, St Giles Cripplegate, All Hallows by the Tower,
St Dunstan-in-the-East.

Spread: Embankment, Strand, Fleet Street, St Paul's, London Wall, Barbican. Good line.

## 6. Under the Pavement: the Working City

Sewers, tunnels, post and power. The bits that were built to be invisible.

Non-obvious insights:
- The tall green iron columns you read as odd lampposts are sewer vent pipes. The
  one in Carting Lane by the Savoy burns gas from the sewer below it.
- The Embankment is hollow. Bazalgette built a main sewer, a railway and a gas main
  inside it and took about 22 acres off the river while he was at it.
- Aldwych station closed in 1994 and its entrance is still on the Strand, in plain
  sight, with the lettering intact.
- Down Street in Mayfair was Churchill's wartime bolthole. The oxblood tiles are
  still on the street frontage.
- The ramp on Southampton Row runs down into the ground and stops. It was the
  Kingsway tram subway.
- The Post Office ran a driverless underground railway from 1927 to 2003 to move
  letters across London.
- The green wooden huts are cabmen's shelters, licensed since 1875. Thirteen are
  left, they still serve food, and only licensed drivers may sit inside.
- The round iron plates in the pavement are coal hole covers, and the panels of
  glass circles next to them are pavement lights, put there to get daylight into
  cellars.

Free sites: this is a street topic and needs no museum. The Postal Museum and Mail
Rail charge, so treat Mount Pleasant as an exterior stop.

Spread: Embankment, Savoy, Strand, Aldwych, Kingsway, Holborn, Clerkenwell. About
2 km.

## 7. Small Marks: Street Furniture and Signs

The topic the whole product idea rests on. Nothing here is famous.

Non-obvious insights:
- Silver dragons mark every road entrance to the City. The two big ones on the
  Embankment came off the old Coal Exchange in 1849. The rest are half-size copies
  made in the 1960s.
- Almost every City street has a parish boundary marker on a wall, usually initials
  and a date at head height. Once you know the shape you see them everywhere.
- Ghost signs are painted adverts still fading on brick, sometimes for companies
  that died a century ago.
- The broad arrow cut into stone at knee height is an Ordnance Survey benchmark,
  a fixed height reference from before satellites.
- The Panyer Boy in Panyer Alley is a 1688 carving claiming to stand on the highest
  ground in the City. It does not.
- Blue police call boxes and posts survive at Guildhall Yard, Piccadilly Circus,
  Aldgate, Walbrook and elsewhere. Good candidates for the soft "have you found it
  yet" prompt.
- Iron cones on old railings are torch snuffers, and the rings are for tying horses.
- The black bollards are not upturned French cannon, whatever you have been told.
  Explaining why the story is wrong is a better stop than repeating it.

Free sites: none needed. St Mary Woolnoth and St Botolph without Bishopsgate work
as shelter stops.

Spread: pan-central. This topic can be laid along any corridor, which makes it the
most useful connector in the set.

## 8. Money, Trade and Empire

Where the money came from, and who paid for it.

Non-obvious insights:
- The Bank of England Museum is free and you can lift a real gold bar. It also
  covers the Bank's own links to slavery rather than avoiding them.
- Lloyd's of London started in a coffee house. So did the Stock Exchange, in
  Jonathan's. The Jamaica Wine House stands on the site of London's first coffee
  house, opened 1652.
- The Royal Exchange has burnt down twice. The third building is a shopping arcade.
  The grasshopper on the weathervane is Thomas Gresham's family crest.
- The Lloyd's building has its lifts, pipes and ducts on the outside so the inside
  can be rebuilt without touching the shell.
- The East India Company ran a large part of India from an office on Leadenhall
  Street. There is nothing left of the building.
- Leadenhall Market sits on the site of the Roman forum, and tourists queue there
  for a Harry Potter photo without knowing either fact.
- Twinings at 216 Strand has been selling tea on that spot since 1706. The shopfront
  is about three metres wide.
- The Beckford statue in Guildhall now carries a re-worded plaque explaining where
  his money came from.

Free sites: Bank of England Museum (free, weekdays, airport-style security),
Royal Exchange, Leadenhall Market, Guildhall Art Gallery.

Spread: Bank, Cornhill, Leadenhall, Lime Street, Aldgate. Tight, about 1 km. The
most compact topic in the set.

## 9. Medicine and the Curious Mind

Science done in ordinary buildings, mostly by people being stubborn.

Non-obvious insights:
- The pump on Broadwick Street is a replica with no handle. John Snow proved cholera
  came from the water there in 1854 by drawing a map. The pub next to it is named
  after him and he was teetotal.
- The Hunterian Museum is free and holds John Hunter's collection. Charles Byrne's
  skeleton was taken off display in 2023 because he had asked not to be dissected.
  That argument is still live.
- The Faraday Museum at the Royal Institution is free, needs no booking, and holds
  the first electrical transformer in the basement lab where it was made.
- Ten chemical elements were first isolated in that one building.
- Newton lived on Jermyn Street. There is a plaque and nothing else.
- Photo 51, the image that showed DNA was a helix, was taken at King's College on
  the Strand.
- Bethlem hospital moved three times. Its third building is now the Imperial War
  Museum, so the building people photograph as a war museum was an asylum.
- Barts has a staircase painted by Hogarth, who did it for free so a foreign artist
  would not get the commission.

Free sites: Hunterian Museum (free, book ahead, Tue-Sat), Faraday Museum (free,
weekdays), St Bartholomew-the-Less. The Old Operating Theatre charges.

Spread: Soho, Covent Garden, Strand, Lincoln's Inn Fields, Holborn, Smithfield.
The Royal Institution in Mayfair is a stretch west and may need dropping.

## 10. Fleet Street: Print, News and Writers

Five hundred years of printing on one street, and none of it left.

Non-obvious insights:
- Every national newspaper was made on or around Fleet Street. None are now.
- Printing came here because the clergy around St Bride's could read. Wynkyn de
  Worde moved Caxton's press next to the church in about 1500.
- St Bride's is the journalists' church, free to enter, with a memorial to reporters
  killed at work and a printing museum in the crypt.
- The Daily Express building at 120 Fleet Street is black glass and chrome from
  1932, and you can see the art deco lobby through the doors from the pavement.
- St Dunstan-in-the-West has a clock from 1671 with two giants who strike the bell,
  and a statue of Elizabeth I carved in her lifetime, moved here from Ludgate.
- The Old Curiosity Shop on Portsmouth Street was named after the Dickens book, not
  the other way round.
- Ye Olde Cheshire Cheese was rebuilt in 1667 and has no natural light in most of it.
- Shops used hanging picture signs because customers could not read, and a few of
  the brackets are still up.

Free sites: St Bride's and its crypt, St Dunstan-in-the-West. Dr Johnson's House
and Temple Church both charge, so use Gough Square and the Hodge statue outside.

Spread: Temple, Fleet Street, Gough Square, Ludgate, St Paul's. About 1 km. The
tightest line in the set.

## Near misses, offered as swaps

- Stage and Song: Bankside theatres, Denmark Street, Soho studios. Strong tourist
  pull, but Bankside and Soho are 3 km apart, which hurts every route it appears in.
- Power and Protest: Tyburn, Smithfield and Wat Tyler, Cato Street, the suffragettes.
  Good material, spread from Marble Arch to Westminster to Smithfield.
- Markets and Eating: Borough, Leadenhall, Smithfield, the gin craze, eel and pie.
  Fun, but the insights are softer and several stops cost money to enjoy.
- Gardens and High Places: St Dunstan, Sky Garden, Barbican conservatory, Inner
  Temple. Lovely, but heavily dependent on opening hours and bookings.

## The structural warning

Three topics combine into one 18-stop route, and all ten combinations have to work.
That makes geographic compatibility a property of the topic *set*, not of any single
topic.

- Pick five topics that all sit in the City and every route walks the same square
  mile. Ten routes will feel like one route.
- Pick five that sprawl across Soho, Southwark and Mayfair and some of the ten
  combinations cannot be walked at all.

The workable middle is five topics that share one corridor. The obvious corridor is
Embankment, Strand, Fleet Street, Holborn and Clerkenwell, St Paul's, Bank, Tower.
About 3.5 km end to end, so an 18-stop route runs roughly 4-5 km of walking.

Topic 7 (Small Marks) is the useful connector, because its stops can be placed
anywhere along the corridor to fill gaps between the other topics' clusters.

## Also worth deciding early

Eighteen stops with reading and looking is three to four hours. That is a full
afternoon, not a stroll. Worth confirming that is intended before any content is
written.
